Was there time for life to start, and to try more than once?

The model follows the chemistry one step at a time and asks, at each step, whether a molecule is passed on before it falls apart. It counts how long each step takes, adds the time needed to grow the community LUCA lived in, and compares the sum with the window between the first liquid water and LUCA. Whatever time is left over tells how many times the whole story could have started and stopped before the one we descend from.

Easy and hard chemistry

What makes a step hard?

A step is hard when its product breaks down faster than the next step can use it, or when the element is hard to get in the first place. The two chemistry choices on the start page use the same rule and the same steps. They only differ in which molecule stands for each step, and in how many successes are needed.

Default and hard, side by side

The default picks, for each step, a molecule with a known easy route. The hard choice swaps three steps for the slowest well-studied molecule of nitrogen, phosphorus and carbon, and asks for a billion successes instead of one. Step S1 (a sulfur compound, H2) is the same in both.

How the cycles were judged

What a cycle does. Each place gets one try per cycle: one drying, one tide, one heat pulse. So the wait for luck is the number of tries needed, divided by the number of places, times the length of one cycle. A cycle twice as fast halves the wait. The cycle enters only this one term, T3.

How long a cycle is. Each length is taken from published estimates for the Hadean, listed below. The fastest (a local hot and cold pulse, about 17 minutes) and the slowest (a pumice raft drifting to a beach, about two years) differ by about 60,000 times. That is the most a change of cycle can do.

Which cycles fit which setting. Not every cycle exists in every place. A deep vent has no wet and dry; a pool has no raft transit. The main and plausible cycles for each setting are a judgement made for this model ★☆☆.

Cycles against surfaces. A surface works differently. It changes the chance that each step passes, and the chances of all steps multiply, so a surface can change the wait for luck by far more than a cycle can. Both only show in the total when the wait for luck is longer than the slow biology and the ecosystem together (about 17 Myr with the defaults). With the default chemistry neither shows. With the hard chemistry both do, and in every setting here the surface changes the wait for luck more than the cycle does.

A second role: the cycle as currency

So far a cycle is only a clock: it says how often a place gets a try. A drying cycle can also pay for a step. Each drying pulls water out so that building blocks can join, and each rewetting can break some of what was made. Switch this on to let the cycle pay for the joining step, the RNA oligomer handoff (H10 free, H11 on a mineral), wherever it is used (step S5 by default).

How it is counted. Each cycle joins a share y of the building blocks and breaks a share d of the product. So one bond takes about cycle length / y to form, and the product survives about cycle length / d, or less if it also breaks down in water between dryings. The chance that a bond is made before it is lost is then close to y / (y + d). For a product with several bonds, the chances multiply.

What changes. Two things. The chance of the joining step now depends on the yield and the loss per cycle, and on the cycle length when the product also breaks down between dryings, as on a slow pumice raft. And the time for the step (part of T2) grows with the cycle length. Cycles that do not dry (local heat pulses, pH and redox at interfaces) do not pay in this way, unless you choose otherwise below.

Which data would set it. One kind of experiment sets both numbers: run the same pool chemistry for 1, 3, 10, 30, 100 and 300 cycles, and measure the yield each time.

The first few cycles give y. The yield climbs by about y per cycle at the start. Eiby 2025 gives one point: after five one-hour cycles at 80 C, 0.1 to 1.2 per cent, with no catalyst (the grey bar).

The level it reaches gives the chance per bond. After many cycles, making and breaking balance, and the yield levels off at y / (y + d). That level is the chance per bond itself, so d follows from it. No one has yet run a series long enough to see it for non-activated nucleotides.

The lengths give the bonds needed. Sequencing the products (nanopore, mass spectrometry) shows how fast the chance falls with each extra unit. The same total time with short and long cycles separates what the cycle pays for from what time alone does.

How T5 is worked out

Two simple rules, both Tier C

New kinds. One kind of cell splits into two at a rate lambda per lineage per Myr, and each new kind keeps splitting. Reaching X kinds takes about ln(X) / lambda. With 5 kinds and lambda 0.1 that is 16 Myr. Modern bacteria split only a little faster than they go extinct, at about 0.03 to 0.05 per lineage per Myr (Louca et al. 2018), and prokaryote lineages grow at a steady rate without filling up (Marin et al. 2017).

New links. X kinds can form X(X-1)/2 pairs. A pair that does not yet interact starts to at a rate r per Myr. To link a share C of all pairs takes -ln(1-C) / r. Half the pairs take 0.7 times 1/r, nine in ten take 2.3 times 1/r, and nearly all take for ever. In the laboratory one cross-feeding partnership evolved within about 800 to 6,500 generations (summary of Helling 1987 and Rozen and Lenski 2000), so years, not millions of years, are a fast upper end.

Together. T5 is the longer of the two (or both added), plus any extra time you choose. Like T4 it is the same for every setting.

How time left and tries are worked out

Read these as orders of magnitude

Time left is the window minus T1 + T2 + T3 + T4 + T5.

One full try is T2 + T3 + T4 + T5, because once a setting exists it stays. With the switch on the left, every new try also waits T1.

Tries that fit is the time open for tries divided by one try, rounded down. One of them is ours; the rest are tries that stopped: runs that could have reached the same complexity and then died out or were overtaken, leaving no descendants.

Chance it fits treats waiting for luck (T3) as random: the wait for n successes follows a gamma distribution with mean T3. All other terms are taken at their central values, so this is a lower bound on the real uncertainty.

Not counted: tries running side by side in different places, which would multiply the number, and the reasons a try stops, which the model does not give.

How to read this

One at a time. Each input moves on its own, from the low to the high end of its plausible range, the same range the sliders use. Inputs that act together (for example the hard chemistry and having no surfaces) can do more together than either does alone.

Four additions. Each one is off, or set as before, until you switch it on, so earlier results do not change by themselves. Every choice below names the papers it rests on, and the stars give our confidence today.

1. When did the clock start?

The start of the window used to be one named event, such as the first sign of liquid water at 4.40 Ga. Here it can be built instead from what happened after the last giant impact: how Earth grew, when the surface froze, and how long it took to take the thick CO2 atmosphere back into the rock.

2. How much water sat on top: ocean depth and dry land

Ingassing also decides how much water stays at the surface and how much goes into the mantle. A deep ocean drowns the continents; a shallow one leaves land. Only settings that need dry land are affected, through the land factor on T1.

3. Boron and the ribose step

Ribose, the sugar in RNA, breaks down within hours to days in warm, alkaline water. Borate binds it and makes it last longer. The default chain has no sugar step, so boron cannot matter until the ribose step is switched on.

4. Delivery from space as a shortcut

Meteorites, comets and dust bring ready-made molecules: nucleobases, amino acids, sugars, HCN and, from interstellar ice, short peptides. Where they land intact they can stand in for one or more steps of the ladder. In the model this is a second route that runs next to making things in place, and the faster of the two shows.

Choose "only by delivery" for a world where, for example, no HCN could form or last, so nucleobases could only come from space.

Minerals, physical conditions and molecular build-up

How a mix of minerals, and how tightly molecules are confined on them, changes what is held, protected and still able to react. How temperature, salt, pH, Mg2+ and porosity act on active sites, binding strength and the activity of dimers and polymers. And a new optional step, molecular build-up: whether a chain can grow long before it is lost.

1. Mineral mix and confinement

Set the weight of each mineral, or start from a preset. Each mineral holds phosphate-bearing molecules with its own binding strength, and the mix adds them up. Confinement is the share of bound molecules held in interlayers, narrow pores or at edge and step sites: better protected, but less free to react.

3. Molecular build-up: from joined units to a useful chain

Joining two units (step S5) is not enough; a chain must grow to tens of units before it can do anything. In each wet and dry cycle a chain adds one unit with chance j, or comes off the mineral with chance exp(-alpha times length), and if it comes off it is caught again with a chance set by how much mineral is around. A chain that is not caught is lost. A bound chain also breaks now and then, less often where it is protected. This follows the model of Alejandre et al. 2025, with mineral loading from Sand et al. 2024 and the amino-acid uptake of Gregoire et al. 2016.

4. Is a tidal zone a good place for build-up?

You asked us to shoot it down if we could. We could not, but it has one serious problem.

For

  • Tides give large wet and dry swings of intermediate period. Alejandre et al. 2025 find that oscillating conditions give chains of 10 to 24 units on average (up to 40), against 1 to 9 under constant conditions. Early tides were 4 to 8 times today's, the day was 10 to 12 hours, and spring tides came every 5 to 7 days.
  • Rivers bring a high load of mineral particles, so a chain that comes off is likely to be caught again on the next grain. A chlorite-rich river plume can strip more than half of the DNA from the water, while other minerals take only about a thousandth (Sand et al. 2024).
  • Where river water meets the sea, salt and Mg2+ or Ca2+ raise adsorption on negative clay faces, so the complex may survive settling (Sand et al. 2024). Edge and step sites hold DNA tighter and protect it better than open basal faces.
  • Once buried, the chain sits in a dense sediment with pore water, where molecules can move from basal faces to edge sites and between minerals.

Against

  • Land. Korenaga 2021 argues that in the late Hadean and early Archean exposed land was limited to isolated ocean islands. Small volcanic islands make small rivers, little felsic sediment and few broad tidal flats. This is the main problem, and it can be tested in card 1 of the timeline page by making land-based settings wait for land.
  • Dilution. Each flood can wash shed chains out to open water, where almost nothing catches them again.
  • Salt competes. Na+ crowds the surface, and above 60 C or below about 100 mM Na+ or 5 mM Mg2+, RNA duplexes come apart (Alejandre et al. 2025). That matters for copying more than for single chains.
  • UV. With no ozone, UV-C reaches an exposed flat (Ranjan and Sasselov 2017). Chains in the top millimetres of mud are shaded; chains on the surface are not.

What the model says now

Our reading: the tidal zone is a strong candidate for the build-up step because it combines the two things the model rewards, drying that drives joining and a high mineral load that catches chains again. Its weak point is not the chemistry but whether enough land, rivers and sediment existed before 4.0 Ga.

Chemistry becomes a probability

Each rung has a transfer time and a loss time. Their ratio is the Damkohler number, Da = tau loss divided by tau transfer. The probability that a species is handed on rather than destroyed is p = Da divided by (1 + Da). That is not an extra assumption on top of the handoff criterion. It is the handoff criterion written as a number between zero and one, so that it can be multiplied across rungs and turned into a waiting time.

A surface holds, protects and lets go

Between S2 and S4 most molecules are not floating free. They sit on a mineral. In the model a surface does three things. It holds a molecule, which is binding affinity. It protects it while it is held, which is stability, so the loss time grows. And it lets it go again, or does not, which decides whether the molecule can still meet a partner and react. Holding helps S2 and S3. For S4 there is a best middle: a surface that holds too tightly, or is already full, gathers molecules but stops them from reacting.

Phosphate is the model molecule. Nucleotides and DNA bind minerals mainly through their phosphate groups, so phosphate is a fair stand-in for anything with a phosphate backbone or a negative charge. The mechanisms follow the work of Karina K. Sand and co-workers at GLOBE: positive surfaces such as iron oxides, carbonates, clay edges and brucite-like layers bind directly, negative surfaces bind only through Mg2+ or Ca2+ bridges, steps and edges bind more tightly than flat faces, and very strong binding can damage what it holds.

Figure M1. How strongly each surface holds phosphate, and how that changes with pH

Distribution coefficient for each surface

Kd is how many litres of water one gram of mineral can strip of phosphate. Multiply it by the grams of mineral per litre: above 1, most of the phosphate sits on the surface. Oxides, green rust and clay edges lose their positive charge as pH rises, so they hold less. Quartz, glass and mica faces are negative throughout and hold phosphate only through Mg2+ and Ca2+ bridges, so their curves are low and flat. Brucite stays positive to about pH 11.

T2 and T3 up close

T1 and T4 are measured in millions of years, so on most figures T2 and T3 shrink to nothing. That hides where the filtering happens. T2 is the chemistry: on every rung a molecule has to be passed on before it falls apart, and the time that takes is minutes to hours. T3 is the waiting for luck: if only some tries get through every rung, the world has to keep trying, cycle after cycle, at every site at once. This tab puts both on their own clock, in seconds, hours and years, so you can see how a change in a chance of passing turns into time.

Add new material

Elements, handoffs and references, as new papers come in

The model reads one JSON file, so extending it does not mean editing the app. Export the full model data, add your entries to the array you want to grow, then load the file back in here. Nothing is overwritten unless an entry has an id that already exists.

A file with a parameters key is read as a saved run. A file with elements, handoffs or references is merged into the dataset.
